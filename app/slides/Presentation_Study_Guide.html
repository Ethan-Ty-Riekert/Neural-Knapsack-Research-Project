4 · Questions and answers

Each answer starts with the one-sentence version, then the depth. Grouped by how a question is likely to arrive.

A · The problem and the model

Q1 · Why is this problem hard?

One sentence: choosing start times and machines for many jobs under capacity limits has an astronomically large number of possibilities, and problems of this family are NP-hard, so no known method is guaranteed to be both exact and fast at scale.

Your report cites Lenstra et al. (1977) for NP-hardness of scheduling problems of this kind; even the single-machine total weighted tardiness problem 1 \mid\mid \sum w_j T_j is strongly NP-hard (verify the exact citation before quoting). A time-indexed exact model has binary variables x_{jmt} for each job, machine and start time: about 100 \times 10 \times 600 \approx 6\times 10^{5} variables with the extended horizon \bar H = H + \sum_j p_j \approx 100 + 500 (order of magnitude), plus |M||R||\mathcal T| capacity constraints. CP-SAT did not reach proven optimality at 100 jobs in 60 s; at 15 jobs it proved 13 of 15 instances optimal.

Q2 · Why lateness squared?

One sentence: squaring makes a few very late jobs cost much more than many slightly late ones, which matches how service agreements work, and it is convex so the penalty grows faster the later a job is.

Linear tardiness cannot tell two schedules apart if they have the same total lateness; squared tardiness prefers the one with the more even lateness. It also has a clean reward form: a late job of weight w_j is charged w_j(2k+1) in its k-th late step, and these add up exactly to w_jT_j^2 (see Maths 2).

Q3 · Why not just use the exact solver (CP-SAT)?

One sentence: it is excellent when it finishes, but at 100 jobs it does not finish, and it needs the whole problem in advance.

Within 60 s it returned schedules worse than both RL and WLST (35,681 against 28,061 and 26,670 offline), so its lower bound could not tell us how far either was from optimal. Online, jobs arrive unpredictably, so an exact solver would have to be rerun at every arrival. A trained policy answers in milliseconds. That speed and the ability to adapt are the motivations for learning, not guaranteed quality.

Q4 · What is the "extended horizon" and why does it matter?

One sentence: no job is ever dropped, so leaving a job unscheduled is always costly, which closes a loophole where agents earned high reward by not scheduling jobs.

The first objective (machines used + weighted tardiness + peak utilisation) could be gamed: unscheduled jobs cost nothing, so agents learned to leave jobs unassigned. That is reward hacking (Skalse et al. 2022). In the final objective, an unfinished job keeps accumulating tardiness until it completes, so the best schedule must finish everything.

Q5 · What is the combined objective and how were the λ weights set?

One sentence: it adds linear lateness, late-job count and energy to the squared-lateness term, and each λ is set so that its term equals J on the best heuristic's schedule, then scaled by 0.25 to 4.

J_\lambda = \sum_j w_j T_j^{2} + \lambda_T \sum_j w_j T_j + \lambda_U \sum_j w_j U_j + \lambda_E E

The calibration \lambda_m = J_{\text{ref}} / x_{m,\text{ref}} (where x_{m,\text{ref}} is the term's value on the best heuristic's schedule) stops a term dominating just because of its units. This is a weighted-sum scalarisation, which can only reach points on the convex part of a Pareto front.

Q6 · Offline versus online, and what is ρ?

One sentence: offline, all jobs are known at time 0; online, jobs arrive randomly, and ρ says how busy the cluster is.

\rho = \max_r \dfrac{\nu\,\mathbb E[p_j a_{jr}]}{|M|\,C_r}: work arriving per step divided by work the cluster can do per step, for the bottleneck resource. Below 1 the queue is stable on average; at or above 1 it grows without bound. ρ = 0.95 is heavy but stable, where scheduling choices matter most (see Maths 11).

Q7 · What exactly is the MDP?

One sentence: the state is everything needed to decide, the action starts a job on a machine or waits, the reward is minus the lateness cost as it accrues, and time advances when a job finishes or arrives.

  • State s_t: waiting jobs and their features (p_j,a_j,d_j,w_j), running jobs with machine, start time and length, remaining capacity R_{mrt}, which machines are on, and the time.
  • Action: A_t=\{(j,m)\}\cup\{\text{idle}\}, with infeasible pairs masked.
  • Transition: deterministic offline; online, random only through arrivals.
  • Reward: negative of the objective, charged as it accrues, plus potential-based shaping.

The Markov property holds because the state contains the processing set and capacities: the future depends on nothing else.

Q8 · What are the offline and online instances like?

Offline: 100 jobs, 10 machines, 4 resources of capacity 30, durations and demands uniform on {1,…,9}, weights on {1,…,5}, deadlines from the Potts–Van Wassenhove tardiness factor TF (0.5 main). Online: Poisson arrivals, log-normal sizes with the same mean, deadline = arrival + slack in {10,…,60}. At ρ = 0.95 that is roughly 11 jobs per time step, about 1,100 jobs over the horizon (see Maths 12).

B · The learning methods

Q9 · What is reinforcement learning, in one breath?

An agent improves a decision rule (a policy) by trying actions, receiving a reward, and shifting probability toward actions that led to higher total reward. There is no list of right answers, only the reward.

Q10 · How does the policy actually learn? (policy gradient)

One sentence: increase the log-probability of each action in proportion to how much better than usual its outcome was.

\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb E\Big[\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\,\big(G_t - b(s_t)\big)\Big]

Full derivation in Maths 4. REINFORCE (Williams 1992) uses the whole-episode return minus a baseline; the baseline lowers variance without adding bias. That is what the "average reward from this step" bars in your demo are.

Q11 · What is the critic, and what is the advantage?

The critic estimates the value V(s), the expected return from a state. The advantage says how much better an action did than the critic expected: \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t). Generalised advantage estimation sums these with a decay \gamma\lambda (Maths 5). The actor is nudged by the advantage; the critic is trained toward what actually happened.

Q12 · PPO versus A2C?

Both are actor-critic. A2C is synchronous and takes one gradient step on each short rollout (20–320 steps in your search space). PPO collects longer rollouts (2,048–16,384) and takes several epochs of minibatch updates, made safe by a clipped objective that stops the policy moving too far in one update (Maths 6). In your results PPO trained online; online A2C did not learn (Option 0 A2C produced the FCFS+FirstFit schedule on all three seeds).

Q13 · What are Options 0 to 4 and why did priority learning win?

They differ in how much the network decides. Priority learning (Options 2, 3) lets the network choose only the job and a fixed rule place it: a small action space (about |J_t|+1 instead of |J_t|\cdot|M|+1 = 1001), and the same split used by DeepRM and Decima. Direct pairs (Option 0) face a 1,001-way choice, which policy-gradient methods find hard (Dulac-Arnold et al. 2015). Rule selection (Option 1) collapsed onto one rule, so it can at best match the best rule in its menu. Action branching (Option 4) splits the product into two heads, about |J_t|+|M| outputs, and was competitive offline but failed online (J = 139,721).

Q14 · What went wrong in training, and how was it fixed?
  1. The critic did not learn. Squared-tardiness returns are in the hundreds to thousands, so value error swamped the shared clipped gradient; the critic explained none of the variance. Fix: divide rewards by a running estimate of the standard deviation of the return (Engstrom et al. 2020; Andrychowicz et al. 2021); explained variance rose to 30–99.9%.
  2. Deterministic evaluation idled. Idle is one action while the probability of starting a job is split over many, so for an undecided policy idle can be the single most likely action everywhere. Fix: a two-stage rule (Maths 9).
  3. Idling repeated forever. Fix: event-driven idling jumps to the next arrival or completion. Combined, validation J of Option 0 A2C fell from 234,116 to 43,164.
Q15 · What is "invalid action masking"?

Actions that would break a capacity limit are removed before the softmax, by setting their logits to −∞, so they get probability 0 and no gradient (Huang and Ontañón 2020). The policy only ever chooses among feasible actions, and training is not wasted on learning that infeasible actions are bad.

Q16 · What is potential-based reward shaping and why is it safe?

Adding F(s,s')=\gamma\Phi(s')-\Phi(s) to the reward gives earlier feedback and provably does not change the optimal policy (Ng, Harada and Russell 1999): the extra terms telescope along any trajectory into a constant that depends only on the start and end states (Maths 3).

Q17 · How were hyperparameters tuned, and why did the defaults often win?

Optuna with the TPE sampler: 24 studies (6 designs × 2 algorithms × 2 cases), 12 trials of 300,000 steps each, median pruning, choice on 20 validation instances. Library defaults were the best configuration in 8 of 24 studies. With only 12 trials in a noisy landscape (one in eight identical Option 2 runs diverged), search rarely beats well-chosen defaults; it is consistent with the known sensitivity of RL to hyperparameters (Eimer et al. 2023).

Q18 · What is the "AI that copies a rule", and why is it in the final results?

Behaviour cloning: a network is trained with ordinary supervised learning to imitate a classic rule's choices, then optionally fine-tuned with PPO. It is a strong test of whether the network and the pipeline can reach rule-level quality at all. Training from scratch must discover the rule by trial and error. Results: a clone lands within noise of its rule; fine-tuning added nothing measurable; scratch was far worse and very variable. See Maths 14. A policy that copies a rule is therefore an upper reference for what training from scratch ought to reach, not a source of new knowledge.

Q19 · Why a window of 20 jobs?

The all-slot network scored over 1,200 job slots, of which about 10 are schedulable and about 30 pending; 88% of the update time went into those slots. Showing only the 20 earliest-deadline schedulable jobs made a 1M-step run about 40× faster (about 30 minutes). A surprise you should state openly: windowed training from scratch did worse than all-slot training, and a larger window did worse still, so the cause is unexplained.

C · Results, statistics and caveats

Q20 · Is a 5.2% gap meaningful? How sure are you?

The gap is outside seed noise (standard deviation across 3 seeds is 219 on a 1,391 gap), so it is not one unlucky run. But three seeds is a small sample, the heuristic WLST was constructed for this objective after RL had already beaten every published rule, and the test set informed some choices. Say: "RL came within about 5%, and I did not beat the best rule."

Q21 · Why do you say "no significant difference" and not "RL matches the rule"?

A confidence interval that includes zero shows the data cannot separate the two; it does not show they are equal. To claim equivalence you need the whole interval inside a margin you chose in advance (for example ±5%) — a two one-sided test (TOST) logic. Your intervals were wider than ±5% for most arms, so equivalence was not shown. That is why the safe phrase is "not significantly different, exploratory".

Q22 · What is "winner's curse" and why did you use fresh instances?

If you pick the best of about 40 heuristics on a test set and then compare against it on the same set, the reference looks better than it really is. Your EDF+Consolidate was exactly that. On 50 new instances (seeds 700000+) the best became MDC+Consolidate at 26,809, and the old "RL from scratch is 20–30% behind" statement turned out to rest on the flattered reference. Fresh data fixes selection bias; it does not fix running many comparisons without correction, which you flag as exploratory.

Q23 · Why does RL win on combined objectives offline?

No single hand-made rule targets the sum of several goals, so there is room for a learned priority; weights matter because the right order then depends jointly on deadline, length and weight (it vanished with unit weights). Care is needed: at moderate λ the winners were trained on J alone, and had fewer late jobs as a side effect of how RL schedules (for example Option 0 A2C: 52.7 late jobs against WLST's 61.7). WLST gives up other metrics to get the lowest J. At the largest weights agents trained on the combined objective took over, which does indicate learning.

Q24 · Why is online harder?

The return depends on a random arrival sequence the agent cannot predict, so the learning signal is much noisier, and episodes are about ten times longer with a thousand-plus job slots. More steps help: Option 2 fell from 39,947 (300k) to 26,515 (1M) to 24,921 (2M, 2 seeds), still 13% behind. A clairvoyant rollout that knows the future arrivals cut J by 70% on three instances, so information, not just training, is a large part of the gap; whether a forecast could capture any of it is untested.

Q25 · Why did RL lose when energy was in the objective?

Options 2 and 3 always place with First-Fit, so they cannot consolidate jobs onto fewer machines, which is what lowers energy; the best energy-aware heuristic uses a Consolidate placement. This is a likely but untested explanation (listed as future work: let RL choose placement).

Q26 · Does tie-breaking matter?

Yes. Deadlines are integers, so several jobs often tie for earliest. On 20 validation instances EDF's J ranged from 28.3k (longest job first) to 36.5k (heaviest first), and the order flipped on the 50 test instances. Differences of about 10% within the EDF family are therefore instance-set noise. A per-slot network cannot see the job index, so index tie-breaking cannot be cloned (agreement stalls near 97%); EDFL (ties by duration) clones at 99.5%.

Q27 · What are the main limitations?
  • Synthetic instances of one size (100 jobs, 10 machines); no real traces yet.
  • Three seeds for most arms; online 2M-step results rest on two seeds; three online tuning studies stopped early.
  • CP-SAT did not reach optimality, so the distance to the optimum is unknown.
  • Choosing the best RL variant on the test set slightly favours RL.
  • Fine-tuned arms used a learning rate of 1e-5 chosen on validation, scratch arms the PPO default 3e-4 untuned, so "scratch is unreliable" is confounded with "untuned".
  • Training-seed variance is not in the bootstrap intervals; no multiplicity correction.
Q28 · What would you do with more time?

Train longer on cloud compute with more seeds; test on Google or Azure traces; let RL choose placement; train one agent conditioned on the objective weights; extract readable rules from the learned priorities (RL for heuristic discovery); test a forecast of arrivals.

Q31 · Why does this matter in practice?

Cloud scheduling decisions repeat millions of times; a few percent in lateness or energy is large in aggregate, and the same structure appears in logistics, manufacturing and hospitals. The honest finding that simple rules are hard to beat is itself useful: a heuristic needs no training and behaves identically every time.

5 · Mathematics behind every model

Each section: statement, derivation, a plain-language line, and where it appears in your project. Items marked verify are from general knowledge and are not cited in your report.

Maths 1 · The scheduling model and the objective

Jobs j\in J have duration p_j, demand a_{jr} for resource r\in R, due date d_j and weight w_j. Machines m\in M have capacity C_r. Binary x_{jmt}=1 if job j starts on machine m at time t.

\sum_{m}\sum_{t} x_{jmt} = 1 \;\; \forall j, \qquad U_{mrt} = \sum_{j}\;\sum_{t-p_j < t' \le t} a_{jr}\,x_{jmt'} \le C_r \;\; \forall m,r,t
s_j = \sum_{m}\sum_{t} t\,x_{jmt}, \qquad T_j = \max(0,\; s_j + p_j - d_j), \qquad U_j = \mathbf 1[T_j > 0]
J = \sum_j w_j T_j^{2}, \qquad J_\lambda = J + \lambda_T\sum_j w_jT_j + \lambda_U\sum_j w_jU_j + \lambda_E E

Here E is total active machine-time, and the constraint inequalities for T_j hold with equality at the optimum because the objective increases in T_j.

Plain: pick a start and a machine for every job so that nothing overloads a machine, and add up how late things are.
Maths 2 · Why the reward adds up to exactly J

A late job of weight w_j is charged w_j(2k+1) in its k-th late step (k=0,1,\dots,T_j-1).

\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}(2k+1) = 2\cdot\frac{(T-1)T}{2} + T = T^2

Induction: true for T=1; if the sum is T^2 then adding 2T+1 gives (T+1)^2. So the total reward is -\sum_j w_jT_j^2 = -J, and maximising return minimises J.

Plain: each extra step of lateness costs more than the last (1, 3, 5, 7…), which sums to the square.
Your project: the demo reward is r_t=-w_jT_j^2/10 for the started job; the real environment charges as lateness accrues.
Maths 3 · Discounting, returns, Bellman equations, shaping
G_t = \sum_{k\ge t}\gamma^{k-t} r_k, \qquad V^\pi(s) = \mathbb E_\pi[G_t \mid s_t=s], \qquad Q^\pi(s,a)=\mathbb E_\pi[G_t\mid s_t=s,a_t=a]
V^\pi(s) = \mathbb E_{a\sim\pi,\,s'\sim P}\big[r(s,a)+\gamma V^\pi(s')\big], \qquad V^*(s)=\max_a\Big[r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)V^*(s')\Big]

Shaping. With r' = r + \gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s), the discounted sum along a trajectory telescopes:

\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t\big(\gamma\Phi(s_{t+1})-\Phi(s_t)\big) = \gamma^T\Phi(s_T) - \Phi(s_0)

so Q'^*(s,a)=Q^*(s,a)-\Phi(s), the ranking of actions in each state is unchanged, and the optimal policy is unchanged (Ng, Harada, Russell 1999). The episodes are finite and the discount is close to 1 (0.99–0.999 searched), so returns stay bounded.

Plain: a bonus for "moving to a better-looking state" is paid back when you leave it, so it only gives earlier feedback and cannot change what is best.
Maths 4 · The policy-gradient theorem, REINFORCE and the baseline

Let a trajectory \tau=(s_0,a_0,\dots) have probability P_\theta(\tau)=\rho(s_0)\prod_t\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t) and total reward R(\tau).

\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\nabla_\theta\sum_\tau P_\theta(\tau)R(\tau)=\sum_\tau P_\theta(\tau)\,\nabla_\theta\log P_\theta(\tau)\,R(\tau)=\mathbb E\Big[\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,R(\tau)\Big]

using \nabla P = P\nabla\log P (the log-derivative trick); the transition terms do not depend on \theta and drop out. Because an action cannot affect earlier rewards, replace R(\tau) by the reward-to-go G_t.

Baseline is unbiased if it does not depend on the action a_t:

\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi}\big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\,b(s)\big] = b(s)\sum_a\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(a\mid s) = b(s)\,\nabla_\theta\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=b(s)\,\nabla_\theta 1 = 0

so subtracting b(s_t) changes the variance but not the expectation. A good choice is b(s)=V^\pi(s), which makes G_t-b an estimate of the advantage.

Softmax policy with linear scores (your demo): \pi(j\mid s)=e^{z_j}/\sum_k e^{z_k}, z_j=\theta^\top\phi_j. Then

\nabla_\theta\log\pi(a\mid s)=\phi_a-\sum_j\pi(j\mid s)\,\phi_j

(the feature of the chosen job minus the average feature under the policy), which is exactly the expression in the demo code.

Plain: make the actions that led to above-average outcomes more likely, and the others less likely. Subtracting the average does not bias this, and it makes it much less noisy.
Your demo: the three weights are time left, length and importance; after training "time left" is strongly negative, so the learner rediscovers least-slack-first.
Maths 5 · Actor-critic and generalised advantage estimation
\delta_t = r_t+\gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1})-V_\phi(s_t), \qquad \hat A_t^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)}=\sum_{l\ge 0}(\gamma\lambda)^l\,\delta_{t+l}

If V_\phi=V^\pi then \mathbb E[\delta_t]=A^\pi(s_t,a_t) (unbiased). \lambda=0 gives the one-step estimate \delta_t (low variance, biased if the critic is wrong); \lambda=1 gives G_t-V(s_t) (unbiased, high variance). Your search space: λ ∈ {0.9, 0.95, 0.98} for PPO and {0.9, 0.95, 1.0} for A2C; the demo uses 0.9. The critic is trained to minimise \mathbb E[(V_\phi(s_t)-\hat G_t)^2].

Explained variance measures whether the critic is learning anything:

\mathrm{EV}=1-\frac{\mathrm{Var}(G-V)}{\mathrm{Var}(G)}

EV near 0 means the critic is no better than a constant (your original fault); EV near 1 means it predicts returns well (after the fix, 30–99.9%).

Plain: the critic is a running guess of "how well will this go from here"; the actor is rewarded for beating that guess.
Maths 6 · PPO's clipped objective, A2C's loss
L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)=\mathbb E_t\Big[\min\big(r_t(\theta)\hat A_t,\;\mathrm{clip}(r_t(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\,\hat A_t\big)\Big],\qquad r_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}

If \hat A_t>0 the objective stops rewarding increases in the ratio beyond 1+\epsilon; if \hat A_t<0 it stops rewarding decreases below 1-\epsilon. Since the gradient vanishes outside the trust region, several epochs on the same rollout are safe. Search space: ε ∈ {0.1, 0.2, 0.3}, epochs ∈ {3, 5, 10}, minibatch ∈ {64, 256, 1024}.

A2C minimises L=-\mathbb E[\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\hat A_t]+c_v\,\mathbb E[(V_\phi-\hat G_t)^2]-c_e\,\mathbb E[H(\pi_\theta)] with entropy bonus c_e\in[10^{-4},3\times10^{-2}] (log-uniform), after each short rollout.

Plain: PPO says "improve, but not by more than a small step in one go", so it can safely reuse data; A2C takes small, frequent steps.
Maths 7 · Action-space sizes and masking
|A|=|J_t|\cdot|M|+1\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;100\cdot10+1=1001 \text{ (Option 0)};\qquad |J_t|+1\le 101 \text{ (Options 2, 3)};\qquad |J_t|+|M| \text{ outputs (Option 4)}

Option 1 chooses among 7 priority rules × 2 placements = 14. Option 3w restricts to the 20 most urgent jobs. Masked softmax: with feasible set \mathcal A(s),

\pi(a\mid s)=\frac{e^{z_a}}{\sum_{a'\in\mathcal A(s)}e^{z_{a'}}}

equivalent to setting infeasible logits to -\infty; those actions get probability and gradient 0. A pointer network scores each job against the state and outputs a distribution over the input items, so the same network handles any number of waiting jobs (Vinyals et al. 2015; Kool et al. 2019).

Maths 8 · Reward scaling

Squared-tardiness returns reach thousands, so the value-regression error dominates a shared gradient. Divide rewards by a running standard deviation of the return: \tilde r_t=r_t/\hat\sigma_G (Engstrom et al. 2020; Andrychowicz et al. 2021). This rescales the loss landscape but not which policy is optimal (a positive constant multiple of the reward leaves the argmax unchanged).

Your project: the single biggest fix; offline Option 0 A2C validation J fell from 234,116 to 43,164 together with the other two fixes.
Maths 9 · The idle action and the two-stage evaluation rule

Idle is one action; "start a job" is spread over many (job, machine) actions. Suppose \pi(\text{idle})=0.20 and ten job actions share the remaining 0.80 equally (0.08 each). The most likely single action is idle, even though the policy starts a job with probability 0.8. Hence the rule:

a=\text{idle}\quad\text{if}\quad \pi(\text{idle}) \ge \sum_{j}\pi(j)
a=\arg\max_{j}\pi(j)\quad\text{otherwise}

For a confident policy this equals plain argmax. Event-driven idling: idle advances time to the next arrival or completion. Waiting between events cannot help: a job that fits at an intermediate step also fits now and finishes earlier, so no schedule is lost. (Idling matters at events because schedules that never idle can exclude the optimum; Giffler and Thompson 1960.)

Maths 10 · The classic heuristics, with the derivation of Smith's rule

Slack \sigma_j(t)=d_j-p_j-t. EDF: smallest d_j. LST: smallest \sigma_j(t). SPT/LPT: shortest/longest p_j. WSPT: largest w_j/p_j. ATC (Vepsäläinen and Morton 1987), as implemented, with k=2 and \bar p the mean duration:

I_j(t)=\frac{w_j}{p_j}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{\max(\sigma_j(t),0)}{k\,\bar p}\Big)

WLST/WEDF (built for this project) scale the slack (or time to deadline) by the weight. MDC (marginal delay cost) is derived from your objective: delaying job j by one tick raises its least possible squared tardiness by

\Delta C_j(t)=w_j\Big[(t+1+p_j-d_j)_+^2-(t+p_j-d_j)_+^2\Big],\qquad \text{start the job with the largest } \Delta C_j/p_j

This applies Smith's ratio rule to the current marginal cost. Smith's rule (1956) is optimal for 1\mid\mid\sum w_jC_j by adjacent interchange: for adjacent jobs i then j starting at time t, swapping them changes the cost by

\big[w_i(t+p_i)+w_j(t+p_i+p_j)\big]-\big[w_j(t+p_j)+w_i(t+p_j+p_i)\big]=w_jp_i-w_ip_j

so i first is better exactly when w_i/p_i\ge w_j/p_j. On parallel multi-resource machines with squared tardiness this is a heuristic with no guarantee, as the code docstring says.

Maths 11 · Load, queues and why ρ = 0.95 is the interesting regime
\rho=\max_{r}\frac{\nu\,\mathbb E[p_ja_{jr}]}{|M|\,C_r}

For an M/M/1 queue (one server, Poisson arrivals, exponential service) the mean waiting time is

W_q=\frac{\rho}{\mu(1-\rho)},\qquad \frac{\rho}{1-\rho}=1,\;3,\;19 \text{ at } \rho=0.50,\;0.75,\;0.95,\qquad \to\infty \text{ as } \rho\to1

(your system is multi-machine, multi-resource and log-normal, so this is only the shape of the effect). Little's law L=\nu W links the average number in the system to the arrival rate and time in system. Your best-heuristic J grows with load roughly as the queue does: about 4,085 at ρ = 0.50, 5,990 at 0.75, 21,986 at 0.95 and 106,463 at 1.10 (old test set).

Plain: waiting grows slowly until the system is nearly full, then explodes; that is why the busy-but-not-overloaded setting is where scheduling choices matter most.
Maths 12 · Generating the instances (Poisson, log-normal, deadlines)

Poisson arrivals with rate \nu: inter-arrival times are exponential with mean 1/\nu. Choosing \nu for a target load: \nu=\rho\,|M|\,C_r/\mathbb E[p_ja_{jr}]. With |M|C_r=10\cdot30=300 and (assuming independent, mean-matched sizes) \mathbb E[p]\,\mathbb E[a]\approx5\cdot5=25: \nu\approx12\rho\approx11.4 jobs per step at ρ = 0.95, about 1,100 jobs over H=100 (illustration; the exact arrival rate is in the code), matching the 1,200-ish slots in the all-slot network.

Log-normal sizes: \ln X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2) gives \mathbb E[X]=e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}. To match the offline mean 5 with \sigma=1: \mu=\ln 5-\tfrac{1}{2}\approx1.11. Many small jobs, a few large ones, like real traces.

Offline deadlines (Potts and Van Wassenhove 1985): d_j\sim U\big[\bar C(1-\mathrm{TF}-\mathrm{RDD}/2),\;\bar C(1-\mathrm{TF}+\mathrm{RDD}/2)\big], floored at p_j, where \bar C=\max(|J|,\;\sum_j p_j a_{jr}/(|M|C_r)) bounds the finish time; larger TF means tighter deadlines; RDD = 0.6.

Maths 13 · The statistics: paired bootstrap, equivalence, selection effects

For arms A and B scored on the same n=50 instances, the paired difference is \bar\Delta=\frac1n\sum_i(J_{A,i}-J_{B,i}). A bootstrap confidence interval resamples the 50 instances with replacement B times, recomputes \bar\Delta, and takes the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. Pairing removes instance difficulty, which is large because squared tardiness is dominated by a few heavy instances.

  • Conditional on the trained seeds: the interval reflects test-instance variation only; training-seed variance is not in it.
  • Significance ≠ equivalence: an interval spanning 0 means "cannot tell". Equivalence within a margin \delta needs (\text{CI}_{\text{lo}},\text{CI}_{\text{hi}})\subset(-\delta,\delta) (TOST logic).
  • Winner's curse: the maximum of many noisy estimates is biased high, so a reference picked on the test set is optimistic.
  • Multiplicity: comparing about 11 arms against 7 heuristics without correction inflates false positives, hence "exploratory".
  • Percent gaps: (RL−H)/H. "RL is 29% below LST" means (39{,}536-28{,}061)/39{,}536=29.0\%; the same fact is "LST is 40.9% above RL".
Maths 14 · Imitation learning: behaviour cloning, compounding error, DAgger
\hat\theta=\arg\min_\theta\;-\mathbb E_{(s,a^*)\sim d_{\pi^*}}\big[\log\pi_\theta(a^*\mid s)\big]

This is supervised cross-entropy on the expert's states. If the learner errs with probability \epsilon on the expert's states, its own state distribution drifts, and the cost over a horizon T can grow as T^2\epsilon (compounding error; Ross and Bagnell 2010) (verify). DAgger (Ross, Gordon, Bagnell 2011) labels the states the learner visits with the expert and retrains, restoring a bound linear in T under conditions (verify); your imitation.py uses DAgger-style labelling.

A per-slot network cannot see the job index, so EDF with index tie-breaks is not a function of the inputs it sees: agreement stalls near 97%; EDFL, which breaks ties by duration, is a function of job features and clones at 99.5%. PPO fine-tuning at learning rate 1e-5 left clones unchanged (higher rates, 1e-4, destroyed them: validation J 37k–52k).

Maths 15 · Hyperparameter search: TPE, pruning, budget

TPE (Bergstra et al. 2011) keeps two density models over hyperparameters x: \ell(x)=p(x\mid y \lt y^*) for good trials and g(x)=p(x\mid y\ge y^*) for the rest, then proposes the x maximising \ell(x)/g(x), which is proportional to expected improvement under its assumptions. Median pruning stops a trial whose intermediate validation J is worse than the median of earlier trials at the same step (only after 5 trials finish). Budget: 24\times12\times300{,}000 steps of tuning in total (before final retraining at 1M steps offline and 300k online, 3 seeds).

Maths 16 · The exact solver: bounds and gaps

CP-SAT returns an incumbent schedule of value U (an upper bound on the minimum) and a proven lower bound L. The relative gap is (U-L)/U; the schedule is proven optimal when U=L. At 100 jobs in 60 s the gap stayed open, so CP-SAT's U could not certify RL or WLST. On 15-job instances the gap closed on 13 of 15 and LST matched the optimum on all of them.

Maths 17 · Weighted-sum scalarisation, Pareto fronts and the trade-off plot

Minimising \sum_k\lambda_kf_k for fixed \lambda\ge0 finds points on the Pareto front's convex part; a point u dominates v if u_k\le v_k for all k with one strict. Your trade-off figure draws the dashed line through the best trade-offs of J against weighted late jobs among heuristics; RL points below it achieve combinations no heuristic reaches. The calibration \lambda_m=J_{\text{ref}}/x_{m,\text{ref}} makes each added term equal to J on the best heuristic's schedule, so a multiplier of ×1 means "this goal counts as much as squared lateness".

6 · Links to other fields

Useful when the visitor is a physicist, a statistician or a control engineer.

Physics

The softmax policy is a Boltzmann (Gibbs) distribution over scores: \pi(a)\propto e^{z_a/\tau}. Maximising expected score plus an entropy bonus, \max_\pi\sum_a\pi_az_a+\tau H(\pi) with \sum\pi_a=1, gives exactly this by a Lagrange multiplier: z_a-\tau\log\pi_a-\tau-\mu=0, so \pi_a\propto e^{z_a/\tau}. The entropy coefficient plays the role of a temperature: high means more exploration, low means near-greedy. This is the same form as the partition function and free energy in statistical mechanics, and the reason simulated annealing and RL exploration look alike.

Control theory

The Bellman optimality equation is the discrete-time, stochastic cousin of the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation of optimal control. RL replaces the known model with sampled experience. Classical baselines such as LQR play the role your dispatching rules play here: strong, model-based, and hard to beat in the cases they were built for.

Statistics

REINFORCE is the score-function (likelihood-ratio) gradient estimator, \nabla\mathbb E_{x\sim p_\theta}[f]=\mathbb E[f\,\nabla\log p_\theta], used for Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis; the baseline is a control variate. Bootstrap, paired comparison and equivalence testing are the tools behind every "not significantly different" in the deck.

Operations research

Dispatching rules are constructive heuristics; CP-SAT is constraint programming with clause learning; a selection hyper-heuristic (Option 1) searches over heuristics instead of schedules. NP-hardness explains why exact methods stop at small instances and why guarantees are rare at scale.
